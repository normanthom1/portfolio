Comparative Health Systems Analysis

Healthcare in the United States and Singapore: A Comparative Analysis

A structured comparison of two high-income health systems: one market-driven and fragmented, one state-coordinated and universal: examining how political economy, governance, and national values shape health outcomes and system costs.

Academic context. Prepared as Assignment One for PUBH 742 (Global Health and International Health Systems) as part of the Postgraduate Diploma in Public Health at the University of Otago (2025). The diploma was completed with distinction overall.

About this project

The United States and Singapore make for an instructive pairing precisely because the contrast is so stark. The US spends roughly $12,400 per capita on health: nearly three times Singapore's $4,300: yet Singapore consistently outperforms it on life expectancy, infant mortality, and preventable deaths. This analysis asks why, and what it implies for how health systems should be designed and governed.

The paper works through both countries across four analytical domains: geography and demographics, socio-economic context, socio-political structure, and health status and issues. It closes with a comparative indicator table and a reflective section on what the comparison reveals about the relationship between governance, values, and health.

Approach

The analysis draws on peer-reviewed literature and international datasets: WHO, World Bank, Commonwealth Fund, and the Lancet, and uses a descriptive-comparative structure to hold each country's system up against the same analytical frame. The goal is not to declare a winner but to understand what each system's architecture produces, and why, given its context.

Both systems are analysed on their own terms before the comparison is drawn, which avoids the common error of judging systems by criteria they were not designed to optimise for. Singapore's system was built for fiscal sustainability and universal access; the US system was built around private markets and pluralist political compromise. Both produce outcomes: the question is which outcomes, for whom, and at what cost.

Key findings

  • The US spends 17.36% of GDP on health against Singapore's 5.57%, yet Singapore achieves a life expectancy of 81.6 years (male) and 86.3 years (female), compared with 73.7 and 79.1 in the US. Singapore's infant mortality rate is 2.1 per 1,000 live births; the US rate is 6.5.
  • The US multi-payer, market-oriented model leaves approximately 30 million people uninsured and produces sharp inequities by income, race, and geography. Medical debt is a leading cause of personal bankruptcy. The Medicaid expansion gap (where around 10 states have still not adopted expansion under the ACA) illustrates how federal structures and lobbying dynamics can block evidence-based reform.
  • Singapore's "3M" system: Medisave (individual savings accounts), MediShield Life (catastrophic insurance), and Medifund (a safety-net for those who cannot afford even subsidised care): achieves universal coverage while maintaining individual financial responsibility. The layered structure redistributes risk without eliminating incentives for cost-conscious use.
  • Singapore's centralised parliamentary governance under long-term PAP rule enables sustained, technocratic health planning that is structurally difficult in the US. This is both an advantage (policy continuity, rapid implementation) and a risk (limited democratic accountability, low tolerance for dissent).
  • Both systems face pressure: the US from political fragmentation, workforce shortages, and rising pharmaceutical costs; Singapore from rapid population ageing, hospital capacity constraints, and growing out-of-pocket affordability concerns despite the 3M architecture.

What this project demonstrates

Health systems analysis at this level requires holding complexity in both hands: understanding that the US system is not simply failing (it leads globally in medical innovation, research output, and specialist care access for the insured) and that Singapore's system is not simply succeeding (its outcomes partly reflect demographic homogeneity, geographic compactness, and political arrangements that would not transfer directly to a pluralist democracy). The skill is in explaining what each system actually produces, under what conditions, and for whom.

This piece demonstrates capability in comparative policy analysis, use of international health data, and the kind of politically grounded, non-idealised systems thinking that applied health analysis requires.
