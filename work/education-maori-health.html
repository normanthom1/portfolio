Health Promotion Analysis

Education as a Determinant of Health for Māori in Aotearoa

A critical four-part analysis of how colonisation and neoliberal education reform produced enduring health inequities for Māori, and how both government policy and Māori-led schooling models function as upstream health promotion interventions.

Academic context. Prepared as Assignment Two for PUBH 713 (Health Promotion) as part of the Postgraduate Diploma in Public Health at the University of Otago (2025). The diploma was completed with distinction overall.

About this project

Education is one of the most powerful social determinants of health. For Māori in Aotearoa, the relationship between educational access and health outcomes has been shaped by more than a century of deliberate colonial disruption: policies that banned te reo Māori, imposed Western curricula, and removed Indigenous communities from control of their children's schooling. The downstream effects on employment, income, housing, and health are well documented. This analysis examines both how those harms were produced and what current interventions, government and community-led, are doing to address them.

The paper is structured in four parts, each applying a different analytical lens: historical and structural analysis, equity-focused policy critique, Indigenous health promotion framework analysis, and critical reflection on approaches to complex health problems.

Part 1: How colonisation and neoliberal reform produced health inequity through education

The Native Schools Act 1867 made English the sole language of instruction and explicitly prohibited te reo Māori in school settings. This was not simply a language policy: it was an assault on cultural identity, social cohesion, and the intergenerational transmission of knowledge that underpins collective wellbeing. The evidence that cultural identity is a protective factor against mental illness, suicide, and substance harm is now strong; stripping it was, in health terms, a population-level risk intervention in reverse.

The 1989 Tomorrow's Schools reforms introduced market competition between schools. For Māori communities (disproportionately concentrated in low-income areas with under-resourced schools), this compounded existing disadvantage. Resource-poor schools could not fund culturally responsive teaching or attract experienced staff, while middle-class flight to higher-performing schools deepened segregation. Lower educational attainment feeds directly into poorer employment, lower income, worse housing, and worse health, alongside psychosocial harms including internalised deficit narratives that affect health-seeking behaviour and chronic stress.

Part 2: Ka Hikitia as an upstream health promotion strategy

Ka Hikitia (Ka Hāpaitia) is the government's Māori education strategy. The analysis assesses it explicitly as a health promotion intervention: one that targets education as a root cause of health inequity rather than addressing downstream symptoms like smoking rates or diabetes prevalence.

Ka Hikitia's strengths include its cultural responsiveness, its explicit alignment with Te Tiriti o Waitangi, and its multilevel approach: addressing individual students, whānau, and the broader system simultaneously. These features align it with contemporary best practice in health promotion, which emphasises upstream, equity-focused, and culturally grounded action.

Its limitations are also significant. Implementation is uneven and in many settings voluntary, meaning schools with the least capacity to change are also least likely to be reached. Structural power over curriculum, funding, and accountability remains with government agencies rather than Māori communities. And the strategy's evaluation framework does not include health outcome indicators: making it difficult to demonstrate or monitor the health gains it is intended to generate.

Part 3: Kura Kaupapa Māori through Te Pae Mahutonga

Kura Kaupapa Māori are Māori immersion schools governed by whānau, operating in te reo Māori, and grounded in tikanga and mātauranga Māori. They are analysed here using Mason Durie's Te Pae Mahutonga health promotion framework, which identifies six components of effective Indigenous health promotion: Mauriora (cultural identity), Waiora (environmental health), Toiora (healthy lifestyles), Whaiora (participation in society), Ngā Manukura (leadership), and Mana Whakahaere (self-determination).

Kura address all six. Daily immersion in te reo and tikanga strengthens cultural identity (the Mauriora component), which is one of the strongest protective factors against poor mental health outcomes in Indigenous populations. Whānau governance structures enact Mana Whakahaere in a way that Ka Hikitia, as a government-designed strategy, structurally cannot. Māori leadership at every level provides culturally grounded role models and signals to students that Māori knowledge and values are not supplementary but central.

Despite demonstrating superior educational and health outcomes, Kura receive less per-student government funding than mainstream schools. This funding inequity limits expansion and concentrates the benefits of community-controlled schooling among communities with the resources and capacity to establish and sustain a Kura. Addressing this is both a health equity issue and a Treaty obligation.

Part 4: What complex health problems require

The comparison between Ka Hikitia and Kura Kaupapa Māori is instructive precisely because both are genuine attempts to address the same underlying problem (Māori educational underachievement and its health consequences) through fundamentally different mechanisms. Ka Hikitia operates through the existing state system; Kura operate outside it, by design.

The lesson is not that government-led strategies are ineffective but that the degree of transformation required to address health problems rooted in colonisation exceeds what can be achieved through cultural sensitivity or consultation alone. Genuine equity requires genuine transfer of power and resources. Tino rangatiratanga (Māori self-determination) is not symbolic. It is functionally essential for effective health promotion with Māori, because the problems being addressed were created, in large part, by the removal of that self-determination.

Epistemological alignment matters too. Western biomedical frameworks that separate health from culture, education, and land are inappropriate lenses for problems that are inherently relational and historically produced. Effective analysis requires the analyst to recognise the limits of their framework and to centre Indigenous knowledge systems rather than accommodate them.

What this project demonstrates

This piece demonstrates capability in social determinants of health analysis, Indigenous health frameworks, equity-focused policy critique, and the kind of historically and politically grounded thinking that effective public health analysis requires. It also reflects a genuine commitment, as a non-Māori analyst, to approaching Te Tiriti-based work with rigour, humility, and respect for the expertise of Māori communities.
