The 2024 smokefree repeal, one year on

The first year of post-repeal data is in. A look at what the modelling predicted, what actually happened, and what a credible restoration pathway would need to look like to be politically durable.

What was repealed

In early 2024 the Coalition Government repealed three tobacco control measures that had been legislated in 2022. The repealed measures were:

  • A reduction in the number of legal tobacco retailers nationally from approximately 6,000 to 600, with restrictions targeted at retailers near schools, marae, and high-deprivation communities.
  • A mandatory 95% reduction in the nicotine content of cigarettes (denicotinisation), intended to reduce the addictiveness of smoked tobacco products.
  • A smokefree generation provision, prohibiting the legal sale of tobacco to anyone born on or after 1 January 2009.

Combined, these measures had been modelled by the Ministry of Health to produce around 580,000 health-adjusted life years and NZ$5–6 billion in long-run system savings, with the largest gains accruing to Māori. They had also drawn international attention as an unusually ambitious tobacco endgame framework, and were the model the UK referenced in proposing its own generational smoking ban.

What the modelling predicted

The modelling work that supported the original framework: work led by researchers at Otago and the Burden of Disease Epidemiology, Equity and Cost-Effectiveness Programme (BODE³): produced a relatively clear set of predictions. Daily smoking prevalence among non-Māori was projected to reach approximately 5.6% by 2025 with the framework in place, and to remain meaningfully higher for Māori (around 11.2%) but converging much faster than under business-as-usual.

Without the framework, the modelling predicted that Māori daily smoking prevalence would not reach 5% until around 2061. That is the metric to keep in mind: 36 additional years of inequitable smoking-related mortality, in exchange for the immediate fiscal effect of the repeal.

What actually happened

The most-cited post-repeal data point is the increase in the estimated daily smoker count between mid-2023 and late 2024: from approximately 284,000 to 300,000, an increase of around 5.6%. This was the first material increase in the daily smoker estimate in decades.

Some care is needed here. A single year is not enough to confidently attribute a population-level smoking trend to a specific policy reversal; cohort sampling variation, measurement changes, and seasonal effects can all contribute to a small move in either direction. But the size and direction of the move are consistent with what the underlying modelling would have predicted from the removal of three reinforcing interventions, and they are inconsistent with the trajectory the country was on for the previous decade.

The cleanest available read is that the policy effect arrived approximately when the modelling said it would.

Why the repeal happened, and why that matters for restoration

The repeal was not, primarily, a public health decision. It was a coalition-formation decision, driven in part by the fiscal value of the tobacco excise revenue the policies would have eroded over time. The estimated NZ$1.5 billion revenue effect of the original framework was treated as a forgone receipt by the incoming government; the modelled long-run health system savings, an order of magnitude larger, sat on a different ledger that was not being weighed in the same calculation.

That is a useful lens for thinking about restoration. The original framework was, in retrospect, institutionally fragile. It passed with a narrow majority on a strongly partisan basis, was tied to a specific government, and had limited explicit cross-party endorsement. When the government changed, the framework went with it.

Any restoration pathway that does not address that fragility will produce another framework with the same vulnerability.

What a durable restoration pathway needs

Three things, at a minimum.

Cross-party engagement before legislation, not after. The original framework had clear scientific endorsement and weak political coalition. A restoration would need the inverse priority order in its early stages: building the political support that will hold the framework in place across one or two electoral cycles, even if that means a slower legislative timeline.

A phased sequencing that builds evidence as it goes. Of the three repealed measures, denicotinisation has the clearest evidence base and the most defensible mechanism. Sequencing it first, with clearly defined evaluation milestones, would allow each subsequent measure to be justified against observed outcomes rather than projected ones. Former Health Minister Shane Reti, in his pre-repeal statements, signalled openness to a denicotinisation-first approach; that political opening is still partly available.

Procedural protections against future reversal. Requiring a public cost-benefit analysis before any future repeal, mandating disclosure of tobacco industry lobbying, and embedding explicit Te Tiriti o Waitangi-grounded review mechanisms would not prevent a future reversal but would raise its political cost. Health policy in an MMP democracy needs to be designed with the assumption that governments will change.

The short version

The 2024 repeal removed a package of measures that had been carefully evidenced and were beginning to look internationally exemplary. The first year of data does not refute the underlying case for those measures; it is closer to a confirmation of it. The work of restoration is not, primarily, the work of re-making the scientific case: that case is already made. It is the work of building a political and institutional coalition that can hold a framework in place across the changes of government an MMP system makes routine.
