After Hauora Māori: looking again at indigenous Medellín

Hauora Māori was one of my favourite papers of the postgraduate diploma. The feedback on my essay said the Aotearoa material was strong but the Colombia material was thin. This is the reflection I did not have room for in the original: what studying Māori housing experience made me see when I looked back at the indigenous Medellín I grew up in, and what Aotearoa is doing that my city could learn from.

Hauora Māori was one of my favourite papers of the postgraduate diploma. I learned more in it than I expected: about Te Tiriti o Waitangi, about Māori experiences of the public health system, about the institutional history that has shaped housing for Māori in Aotearoa, and about how to think about wellbeing through frameworks that did not come from where I had been trained.

Part of the feedback on my essay was that the writing was strong on the Aotearoa material and the international comparison with Canada and Australia, but a bit thin on the country I am actually from. I want to use a blog post to do what the essay did not have room for: bring Medellín forward, and put it next to what Aotearoa has been doing.

I want to be honest from the start. This is not an academic comparison. The essay was that. This is a reflection: the kind of thinking that happens after the marking is done, when the framework you learned has stopped being a framework and become a way of looking.

What Hauora Māori made me see

The single thing I have not been able to stop thinking about is the framing of housing as a hauora issue rather than a property issue. Te Whare Tapa Whā, the four-cornered model of health developed by Sir Mason Durie, treats wellbeing across four taha: wairua (spiritual), tinana (physical), hinengaro (mental and emotional), and whānau (extended family). The essay I wrote worked through how housing inequities propagate through every one of those: taha tinana through respiratory illness in cold and damp homes, taha hinengaro through the chronic stress of housing insecurity, taha whānau through overcrowding and the disruption of intergenerational living, taha wairua through severed connection to whenua, ancestral land.

Before I sat that paper, I had been taught to think about housing primarily as a determinant of physical health: damp causes respiratory disease, overcrowding spreads infection, cold raises cardiovascular risk. That framing is correct as far as it goes. What Te Whare Tapa Whā made me see is that it is also too small. The damage housing does, and the healing better housing offers: is spiritual, relational, and psychological as much as it is bodily. The Māori-led programmes I wrote about (papakāinga housing on ancestral land, and the Whai Kāinga Whai Oranga investment in Māori-led delivery) make more sense once you see this. They are not housing programmes that happen to be culturally responsive. They are hauora programmes that take built form.

That is the lens I have not been able to set down. It is also the lens I want to bring back to Medellín.

Indigenous Medellín, for readers who do not know it

Colombia has been, by its own constitutional definition, a pluri-ethnic and multicultural nation since 1991. The country recognises around one hundred and fifteen indigenous nations and approximately 1.9 million people who identify as indigenous (the 2018 census). The constitutional framework is in some respects stronger on paper than what Te Tiriti has been able to achieve in New Zealand statute: resguardos (constitutionally protected communal indigenous territories), consulta previa (a legal requirement to consult indigenous communities on projects that affect them), and explicit indigenous autonomy provisions. In practice, it is honoured unevenly, and almost none of its protections were designed for the indigenous families who have been displaced from their territories into cities.

Medellín is one of those cities. The displacement that brought indigenous Colombians here is mostly conflict-driven. The Emberá, in particular (both Emberá Katío and Emberá Chamí), were pushed from their territories in Chocó and Risaralda by sustained armed conflict, paramilitary activity, and resource-extraction pressure from the mid-2000s onward. Members of around twenty indigenous nations now live in Medellín. Some are concentrated in particular neighbourhoods; many are dispersed across the comunas, often in informal settlements on the hillside edges of the city.

The Emberá story has been particularly visible. Families displaced to Medellín and Bogotá have at times ended up in paga-diarios: pay-by-the-night tenements where several families share a single room, or in street encampments in central public spaces. There have been return programmes: in recent years, sixty-one Emberá families went home from Medellín to the Alto Andagueda sector of Bagadó municipality with support from the Unidad para las Víctimas, Colombia's Victims Unit. The return numbers are small relative to the scale of displacement. The point is not that everything is working. The point is that Medellín has been engaging with this, not perfectly, not at full scale, but with a deliberateness that did not exist before.

What Medellín has been doing

The most important indigenous institution in my city is the Cabildo Indígena Chibcariwak. The name is a compound: Chibcha, Caribe, and Arawak, three of the linguistic and cultural families that make up indigenous South America. The cabildo was founded in 1979, which makes it older than the 1991 Constitution that recognised it nationally and makes it older than almost any urban indigenous organisation of its scale on the continent. It currently registers more than two thousand indigenous people in its membership databases and brings together members of at least a dozen indigenous nations: Emberá, Inga, Camsá, Kuna, Quichua, Zenú, Nasa, Guambiano, Pasto, Siona, Arhuaco, Wayúu, among others. The cabildo functions as a self-governance, advocacy, and cultural-transmission body inside the city, and operates under a Plan de Vida, a Life Plan, which is the formal document that a Colombian indigenous community develops in dialogue with the state to set its own priorities on its own terms.

Eight indigenous cabildos now operate in Medellín. The fact that there are eight rather than one is itself a piece of policy: it reflects how diverse urban indigenous Medellín has become, and how seriously the city has taken indigenous self-organisation as a legitimate political form rather than as something to be subsumed into a single welfare programme.

The Medellín Alcaldía runs a Gerencia Étnica, an ethnic affairs office, which coordinates programmes for the more than 250,000 indigenous, Afro-Colombian, Black, raizal, and palenquera people living in the city. The office runs initiatives including Casa a Casa Étnico, a house-to-house outreach programme, and a mobile indigenous service office that takes services into the comunas rather than asking residents to find their way through the central administration. Through these and other programmes, the Gerencia Étnica reports having reached more than eighteen thousand people. Specifically indigenous housing programmes are smaller in scope than the city's general housing programmes, but they exist: the Alcaldía currently houses twenty-four indigenous university students directly through the Gerencia's housing route, and supports the eight cabildos with operational funding for the cultural and economic projects they identify in their own Life Plans.

The wider context matters too, even though it was not designed as indigenous housing policy. Medellín's social urbanism transformation: the Proyectos Urbanos Integrales, the Metrocable cable cars that connect hillside comunas to the city below, the Parques Biblioteca, the Comuna 13 escalators, the dramatic neighbourhood upgrading from the early 2000s onward: was citywide poverty intervention. But the indigenous and Afro-Colombian families who live in those neighbourhoods received the benefit of it. Between 2002 and 2010, the city's poverty rate fell by 39%. The homicide rate, which sat at 395 per 100,000 in 1991: the worst in the world by some distance: is now in the low double digits. That kind of structural improvement does not solve indigenous urban housing on its own, but it is the soil out of which more targeted work has been able to grow.

What Medellín could learn from Aotearoa

Three things travel.

The first is the papakāinga model. Papakāinga (literally a nurturing place to return to) is communal housing developed by whānau on whenua Māori, typically a cluster of three to ten homes operating as an intentional community under kaupapa Māori. Te Puni Kōkiri provides capital grants for infrastructure and construction; the housing is owned collectively by the people who whakapapa to the land. The structural fit with Colombian resguardo land is obvious, and the absence of an equivalent Colombian programme is one of the clearest gaps in our indigenous urban housing policy. A Colombian papakāinga equivalent (communal housing developed on land held by or for an urban cabildo, designed by the community itself, funded by the state) would not solve everything, but it would create something Colombia does not currently have: an indigenous-led housing form that the state finances but does not design.

The second is Whai Kāinga Whai Oranga, the four-year, $730 million programme that ran from 2021 to May 2025 to accelerate Māori-led housing delivery. The specifics of the programme matter less than the structural choice it represented: a major capital investment whose explicit purpose was to strengthen the capability of Māori organisations to deliver housing themselves, rather than to deliver housing to Māori people. The distinction sounds small. It is not. Colombian indigenous programmes have tended to be designed as services that the state delivers to indigenous communities. The Aotearoa direction of travel, capability funding for indigenous-led delivery, is structurally different, more durable, and worth importing.

The third is harder to operationalise but important to name: Te Whare Tapa Whā treated as a housing design framework, not only a health framework. The same insight applies in Colombia, where indigenous frameworks like the Buen Vivir tradition (with roots across Andean indigenous philosophy) are taken seriously inside indigenous communities but rarely make it to the front of housing programme design. The work of putting indigenous wellbeing frameworks at the design stage of housing programmes, not at the evaluation stage, is intellectual and structural rather than financial, and Aotearoa is further along that road than Colombia is.

What I have come to think

There is a small and stubborn moment in studying another country's indigenous experience when the framework you have been using on someone else's situation turns back and asks you about your own. Hauora Māori did that for me. I went into the paper knowing about Te Tiriti o Waitangi as a piece of history. I came out of it understanding partnership, protection, and participation as live tests that any honest analyst should apply to indigenous policy anywhere, including in the city I grew up in.

Medellín is doing more than it gets credit for. The Cabildo Chibcariwak is approaching half a century of work. The Alcaldía's ethnic affairs structure is serious and growing. The famous social urbanism transformation, for all its limits and all the criticism it has rightly attracted, materially improved the comunas where indigenous and Afro-Colombian families live. The gap is not absence of intention. It is the structural one of designing indigenous-led housing rather than indigenous-targeted housing, and that is where Aotearoa is doing work worth knowing about.

I came back from the Hauora Māori paper with a sharper way of looking at my own country. That, in the end, may be the most useful thing studying anywhere does.
