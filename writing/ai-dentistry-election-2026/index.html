Opinion · Election 2026 · 1 November 2026

AI can make dental care faster. It cannot decide who pays for it.

New Zealand votes on Saturday 7 November. Free dental care is one of the real choices at this election, and AI is arriving in our clinics at the same time. After ten years in Wellington dental practices, I think voters should look at both together.

The short version, if you are voting this week

  • For adults, dental care in New Zealand is mostly private. Some parties want to change that, others do not.
  • The biggest argument against free dental care is the lack of dental workers. AI is being offered as a way to do more with fewer people.
  • AI can help a clinic work faster, but only for people who can already pay to be in the chair. Who pays is a political decision, and on 7 November it is ours.

This is not about who to vote for. It is about what we should ask before we vote.

Teeth are outside the system

For most of the past ten years I have worked as a dental assistant in Wellington. I hand the instruments, take notes, and talk with patients when they are nervous.

Very often I see the same moment. The dentist explains the treatment plan, the patient looks at the price, and then asks which part can wait.

This is not because people do not care about their teeth. In New Zealand, teeth are mostly outside the public health system.

Children and teenagers get free basic dental care until they turn 18, through a public service that dental advocates say is already under pressure. After 18, you pay. If you are on a low income and have an emergency, you can apply to Work and Income for a grant of up to $1,000, raised from $300 in 2022. That helps with pain. It does not pay for regular care.

The data shows it. In the 2024/25 New Zealand Health Survey, about 1.8 million people said they needed dental care in the past year but could not get it because of cost. For Māori and Pacific people, the rates are higher again.

In the clinic, I only see the people who made it to the chair. Most of the 1.8 million I never see.

What is on the ballot this time

Dental care is one of the clearest differences between the parties.

  • The Green Party is promising free, publicly delivered dental care for everyone.
  • Te Pāti Māori has free dental care in its platform too.
  • The Opportunity Party is promising free dental care for people under 30.
  • Labour's health offer is about free GP visits. Dental advocates say it does not include dental care this time.
  • I could not find a 2026 dental care policy from National, ACT, or New Zealand First. If I missed one, please tell me and I will update this piece.

You can compare the parties' health policies at whichparty.nz.

The main argument against free dental care is not only money. It is the workforce. Who will do all this extra work?

It is a fair question. The New Zealand Dental Association's 2026 report card shows 15.6% of dentist positions in public oral health services are vacant, and 9.5% of oral health therapist positions. There are only 60 government-funded places for new dentistry students each year.

From inside the clinic, I can say the shortage is real. It is not just a number in a report.

Where AI comes in

Across the health system, AI is being offered as one answer to the workforce problem.

The clearest example is in hospitals. In October 2025 the government announced AI scribes for emergency departments. These tools listen to the consultation and write the clinical notes. By March 2026, an AI scribe was live in every emergency department, used by 1,250 clinicians. In the pilots, doctors saw about one extra patient per shift on average. The pilots were small, but they show the idea: less time writing, more time with patients.

Dentistry is not far behind. AI software that reads dental x-rays was cleared by Medsafe in 2021, and the evidence is good. In a UK study, dentists using AI found more early tooth decay in x-rays than dentists without it. In another, the AI was more accurate than the average dentist at finding decay between the teeth: 80% against 71%.

Scribe tools could also take over part of my own job, writing down what the dentist says during the exam.

I think this is good news. Finding decay earlier is better for patients. Less time on notes is better for everyone in the room.

But this is where I start to worry.

Finding more decay is not the same as treating it

When AI finds more early decay, someone has to decide what to do about it.

A lot of very early decay, the kind that is only in the enamel, can be stopped without drilling: fluoride, changes in diet, better cleaning, and checking again at the next visit. No filling needed.

The study I mentioned above makes this point. It found that AI is good value for money, but only if the early lesions it finds are managed without fillings. If every new spot becomes a filling, the benefit is much smaller and the patient pays more.

In a private system, where adult care is paid treatment by treatment, this matters. A filling has a price. A conversation about sugar and fluoride is worth much less to a practice.

Most dentists I have worked with are careful and honest. This is not about them. It is about how the system is built, and systems shape decisions.

There is a second problem. AI only helps patients who are already in the chair. People who cannot afford to come do not get their x-rays read by AI. They do not get x-rays at all.

In public health we call this the inverse care law. In 1971, the British doctor Julian Tudor Hart wrote that good care tends to be least available to the people who need it most. New technology can make this worse if it only arrives where the money already is.

Faster is not the same as fairer

So what does this mean for the election?

I think AI can help with the workforce problem. If a dental team spends less time on notes and finds problems earlier, it can see more people. That makes free dental care a little more realistic than a few years ago.

But AI does not decide who those people are.

If adult dental care stays private, the time AI saves will mostly go to the patients who already come. With public funding, the same tools could help a small workforce reach people who are not coming now.

The technology is the same in both cases. The difference is who pays, and that is not a technology question. It is a political one.

A view from Colombia

I trained as a dentist in Medellín, so I always compare.

In Colombia, basic dental care like fillings and extractions is part of the public health benefits plan. That does not mean everyone gets it. Researchers at my old university, Universidad de Antioquia, have shown that access is still a big problem. But at least teeth are inside the system.

It still surprises me that in New Zealand, a much richer country, adult teeth are outside it.

Questions I would ask before voting - if you’re having a toothache deciding.

If you are still deciding, or a candidate knocks on your door this week, these are the questions I would ask:

  1. Will your party fund dental care for adults, and for who? Everyone, people on low incomes, people under 30, or nobody?
  2. If you promise free dental care, where will the workers come from? 60 new dentistry students a year is not enough.
  3. Is AI part of your plan, and who checks that it is safe? These tools are new in clinics, and many are made by overseas companies that hold our health information.
  4. Will AI be used to reach people who are not coming to the dentist now? Or only to make appointments faster for people who can already pay?

The answers will be different for each party. That is the point of an election.

The part AI cannot do

AI can help us find decay earlier. That is a good thing.

But finding decay is not the same as treating it. And treating it is not the same as paying for it.

That last part is a choice we make together.

On Saturday, we make it.

~
