What the Wellington fluoridation under-dosing review actually means

A 2025 review found that parts of Wellington's water supply had been inadequately fluoridated for several years. A short explainer of what that finding does and does not show, who it most affects, and what the regulatory implications are.

What happened

In 2025 a review found that Wellington's water had been inadequately fluoridated for six years. Fluoride levels at parts of the city's water treatment infrastructure had been running below the recommended 0.7–1.0 mg/L target during that period.

The story attracted a particular kind of public attention. Fluoridation is one of the more polarised topics in New Zealand public health discourse, with a small but vocal cohort opposed on principle, and a much larger group whose views could best be described as inattentive. A finding that the water had been under-fluoridated for years sat awkwardly between those positions: not what the most vocal opponents had warned about, not what the broader public had been told to assume was happening, and not obviously easy to interpret as either a vindication or a failure.

What it does show

The straightforward reading is that the regulatory system, in this specific case, did not detect a sustained drift away from the recommended fluoride concentration over a six-year period. That is a finding about the regulatory system, not about fluoride.

New Zealand's fluoridation policy assumes the system can be set, monitored, and held within a narrow band: 0.7 to 1.0 mg/L is the target range, well below the 1.5 mg/L WHO upper safety limit. The health effects of fluoridation are well-characterised at those concentrations. The point of fluoridation as a public health intervention is precisely that it is hands-off: a population-level dose delivered through infrastructure, not behaviour. That model only works if the infrastructure is doing what we assume it is doing.

The Wellington finding is a reminder that the model has a third leg (assurance) that has to function in order for the other two to deliver the population health effect we expect.

What it does not show

It does not show that fluoride at recommended levels is harmful. The 2014 Royal Society of New Zealand review, the Office of the Prime Minister's Chief Science Advisor review, and the 2023 update all reach the same conclusion: fluoride added to drinking water at the New Zealand target concentration has not been shown to cause adverse systemic effects, and the protective effect on dental caries is well-evidenced.

It also does not show that fluoridation is unnecessary. The opposite is closer to true. A period of under-fluoridation would be expected to produce some additional caries burden in the population, and the populations who feel that burden most are the populations for whom fluoridation matters most: children, people with limited access to dental services, and Māori and Pasifika populations who already carry higher rates of dental disease.

Who feels it

Caries develops over years. A six-year period of under-fluoridation will not show up as a single shock in oral health data; it will show up as a slow widening of pre-existing inequities. That is the actual story underneath the headline.

Year 8 oral health data already shows Māori and Pasifika children are less likely to be caries-free in regions without effective fluoridation than in regions with it. The protective effect of fluoridation is strongest precisely for the populations who have the least access to private dental care: which is the same population for whom the disappearance of that protective effect is hardest to make up for.

The regulatory implication

The 2021 Health (Fluoridation of Drinking Water) Amendment Act moved decision-making authority from local councils to the Director-General of Health. That centralisation responded to the difficulty of getting consistent fluoridation decisions made under local political pressure. The Wellington under-dosing finding does not undermine that centralisation; if anything, it sharpens the case for sustained central oversight of monitoring and assurance, not just policy.

The 2025 proposal to revert decision-making to local referenda would not have prevented the Wellington under-dosing: that was an operational failure within an existing fluoridation regime. But it would, over time, produce a much more fragmented and harder-to-monitor national picture, and would be likely to leave the populations with the highest oral disease burden in the regions with the least consistent protection.

The short version

Wellington's water was under-fluoridated for longer than it should have been. The lesson is that monitoring matters as much as policy. The implication is not that fluoridation has failed: it is that we should be much more attentive than we have been to whether the infrastructure is actually doing what the policy is meant to make it do, and to who pays the price when it does not.
