Los Cafeteros, the World Cup, and the quieter game being played in Colombia

With the 2026 World Cup days away and Colombia returning to the tournament after missing 2022, a different kind of football story is unfolding back home: in former conflict zones, displaced communities, and street fields in cities most of the world has never heard named. A note on football as a public health intervention, and what it has been doing for the country that taught me to love the game.

In Colombia, football is huge. I grew up watching it with my family, and I have always supported our national team: Los Cafeteros, the coffee growers, a nickname Colombia adopted in the 1980s when our coffee was as known abroad as anything else about us. With the 2026 World Cup arriving in a few weeks and Colombia qualified for the first time since 2018, the matches will be on in our house in Wellington as they will be in living rooms across Colombia. So I thought I would write something about football.

The angle I want to take is not the one I expected when I started thinking about it. Most public health writing about football looks at injuries, cardiac events during matches, alcohol consumption around tournaments, or domestic violence rates during games. All of those matter and there is real data on them. What I want to write about is quieter. In the years since Colombia's 2016 peace agreement, football has been doing something in my country that is worth describing in public health terms, even though it does not look like a public health intervention from the outside. It looks like children playing on dirt fields in towns most of the world has never heard named. But what they are playing, and what is happening around the games, is a piece of how Colombia has been rebuilding after fifty years of war.

A very short version, for readers who do not already know the context

Colombia's armed conflict lasted more than fifty years. The largest of the armed groups was the FARC: Fuerzas Armadas Revolucionarias de Colombia, the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia: a Marxist guerrilla organisation founded in 1964 that fought the Colombian government across rural Colombia for decades. The conflict drew in other actors as well: right-wing paramilitaries, drug cartels, smaller guerrilla groups, and the state itself. At least 220,000 people were killed. Close to six million Colombians had been displaced from their homes by the time the peace process began, mostly into the urban edges of cities like Bogotá and Medellín, and the displacement crisis has continued: UNHCR now counts around seven million internally displaced people in Colombia, making it the fourth-largest internal displacement crisis in the world today.

In 2016, after four years of negotiations in Havana, the Colombian government and the FARC signed a peace agreement. President Juan Manuel Santos won the Nobel Peace Prize that year for his role in negotiating it. More than 6,800 FARC fighters laid down their weapons and entered a formal reintegration process. The peace has been imperfect: implementation has been slow, dissident armed groups have appeared in some former FARC areas, and forced displacement continues, but the agreement is the foundation of a real, ongoing transition. Colombia in 2026 is a different country from Colombia in 2002, even where it is still working out what kind of different.

I will not pretend that football has fixed any of that. It has not. But it is doing something useful in the spaces the war left behind, and that something is what this essay is about.

What football is doing in the spaces the war left behind

A 2014 international survey reported that 94% of Colombians were interested in that year's World Cup: the highest figure of any of the nineteen countries surveyed. That kind of cultural reach matters when you are designing programmes for vulnerable young people, because it means the front door of a youth programme can be a football, and almost everyone will walk through it.

In the years since the peace agreement, several organisations have built programmes on exactly that insight. The two I want to describe are Tiempo de Juego and Colombianitos.

Tiempo de Juego: Game Time: is a foundation working with around six thousand children and adolescents across three regions: Bogotá's poorer outskirts, Timbiquí on the Pacific coast (one of the regions most affected by displacement and ongoing armed-group activity), and Magdalena in the north. The foundation uses football as the convening activity, but the work that happens around it is broader: life skills, gender equality, civic values, music, the arts. As a member of the FIFA-affiliated streetfootballworld network, it applies a methodology called football3, structured around three halves: a pre-match discussion where players agree the rules together, the match itself, and a post-match discussion where they reflect on what happened. There is no referee. Points are awarded for fair play as well as goals. The whole point is to make the game itself the teaching environment, rather than a thing that happens around teaching.

Colombianitos runs a similar approach at larger scale. Its Goals for a Better Life programme operates in seventeen Colombian municipalities: many of them in areas affected by the armed conflict, and reaches around twenty-five thousand young people. A 2024 paper in Frontiers in Sports and Active Living traced its impact on participants and documented changes in conflict resolution, non-violent communication, and life-skill development.

Both of these foundations operate inside the same logic. Football provides the reason to show up. The structured discussion around the football provides the intervention. The relationships built between players, coaches, and the community provide the duration. Nothing about it requires the children to think of it as health work. That is part of why it works.

There is also a more symbolic kind of programme. La Paz F.C. is a Bogotá amateur football club, founded in 2017, that fields ex-FARC combatants alongside victims of the conflict, members of other armed groups, and people from communities directly affected by the war. It was set up by a human rights lawyer, Félix Mora, through the Fundación Fútbol y Paz (Football and Peace Foundation). The squad has FIFA's acknowledgment and ambitions to reach Colombia's second division. The point of the team is not really to compete; it is to put people who once tried to kill each other in the same jersey, on the same field, and let them be teammates in front of an audience. Reconciliation made publicly, weekly.

Why a public health analyst is writing about this

When I describe these programmes to friends in Wellington, the question I am most often asked is: this is lovely, but is it public health?

The answer depends on what you mean by public health. If you mean clinical services delivered by health professionals: no. If you mean structured interventions aimed at improving the health of populations rather than individuals, with strong attention to the social determinants of health: yes.

The young people these programmes work with are at the steep end of every public health indicator that matters. They live in places with high rates of community violence, school dropout, early pregnancy, substance use, recruitment by armed groups, and untreated mental ill-health. Many are children of displaced families. Many have lost family members to the war. The traditional public health system reaches them poorly, partly because the formal system is concentrated in cities and partly because trust in state-affiliated institutions is, for understandable historical reasons, low in these communities.

Football reaches them. That is not a small thing. A youth programme that can credibly recruit and retain young people in those communities is doing work that direct-service health interventions struggle to do at all. The benefits documented in the literature (improved conflict resolution, social cohesion, life-skill development, psychosocial support, reduced exposure to armed-group recruitment) sit squarely inside what public health calls upstream intervention: changing the conditions in which people grow up so that fewer of them present at the downstream end of the system with problems that are very expensive to fix.

There is also the simpler version of the same point. A 2016 sport-for-development study in Cazucá, an urban settlement of displaced people outside Bogotá, described how participation in structured football reduced young people's day-to-day exposure to street violence simply by giving them somewhere else to be at the times of day when they would otherwise have been on the street. That is not glamorous. It is also not nothing.

The Colombian government has built on this thinking. After the peace agreement, the Ministry of Sport hired ex-combatants directly as Monitores de Paz: Monitors of Peace: to run sport and recreation programmes inside the ETCRs (Espacios Territoriales de Capacitación y Reincorporación), the formal reintegration zones where demobilised FARC members began the transition to civilian life. The framing was simple: ex-combatants who were not finding traction in civilian life were at risk of being pulled back by dissident armed groups, and giving them paid work running youth sport programmes in their own communities was both a livelihood for them and a useful contribution to the communities they were rejoining.

What the evidence actually says

I want to be careful here. Sport-for-peace work in Colombia is real and well-documented descriptively, but the academic literature evaluating its public health impact is still developing. Recent reviews describe the field as having a growing body of qualitative evidence on behavioural and social outcomes, and a smaller body of quantitative work on population health indicators. The 2024 Frontiers in Sports and Active Living paper on Colombianitos is one of the stronger evaluations, and even there the strongest evidence sits at the level of participants and immediate communities: improved conflict resolution, stronger social capital, healthier coping strategies: rather than at the broader population level.

That is normal for upstream interventions. The benefits accrue over years, in many places at once, in populations who are also affected by lots of other things at the same time. The same evaluation problem exists for Aotearoa's Whānau Ora programmes and for indigenous-led early-childhood initiatives in Australia. The question is not whether the work is doing nothing: it clearly is not, but how much of what it is doing can be cleanly measured, and on what timescale.

What I will say is this: the people running these programmes know things about how to reach young people in post-conflict communities that the formal health system does not. That knowledge is itself a public health asset and it is worth protecting.

As the World Cup approaches

Colombia qualified for the 2026 World Cup with a top-six finish in CONMEBOL qualifying, returning to the tournament after missing 2022. By the time this is published, the matches will be days away. In our family, in Wellington, we will be watching. Los Cafeteros will play in front of a country that has, in living memory, been one of the most violent places on earth, and is now somewhere different: not safe everywhere, not finished with its long peace process, but somewhere different.

What I find underrated is that the country watching with us is also a country where, in dozens of towns most of the world will never hear named, children will be playing the same game on dirt fields in programmes that have been quietly putting football to work as an instrument of public health. The tournament is loud. The other work is quiet. Both matter, and both are forms of the same culture finding ways to be useful to itself.

¡Vamos Colombia!
