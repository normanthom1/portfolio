Ten years as a dental assistant: what I saw about oral health inequity in Aotearoa

A decade in Wellington dental practices is a particular vantage point on New Zealand's oral health system. The cost-of-care barrier is not abstract from the chair-side; neither is the workforce shortage. What that vantage point shows about who falls through, and why oral health needs to sit closer to the centre of public health policy debate.

The first thing you learn working in a Wellington dental practice is that most New Zealanders do not come to the dentist for a check-up. They come because they are in pain. By the time someone makes that appointment, they have usually been carrying the problem for months: often longer.

This is not how dental care is supposed to work. The model that public oral health is built on assumes regular preventive visits, modest interventions when problems are caught early, and minor maintenance over a person's lifetime. When that model collapses, what arrives in clinics is the consequence: extractions that could have been fillings, root canals that could have been small restorations, abscesses that could have been routine appointments two years earlier.

I have spent ten years watching that collapse from inside.

Why the model collapses

The simplest part of the explanation is cost. Adult dental care in New Zealand is not funded by the public health system in any meaningful way. A check-up and clean costs $150 to $300 at most Wellington practices. A filling is $200 to $400. A crown is $1,500 to $2,500. Root canal treatment runs into the thousands. For someone earning around the minimum wage, a single tooth problem can cost a week's pay or more, and that is before they have factored in the fact that taking a half-day off work also costs them money.

The result is rational: people defer. They take painkillers. They wait. They hope it resolves. Sometimes it does: many dental pains do recede temporarily, which makes the deferral feel justified in the moment and worse in the long run. By the time someone presents to a clinic, the cheap interventions are off the table.

The second part of the explanation is workforce. Dentists, dental therapists, and dental hygienists are all on the Immigration Green List because we do not have enough of them, and the gap is sharper outside the major centres. That is true at the system level. At the practice level, what it means is that even people who have decided they want care, and have the money for it, can wait months for an appointment with a particular clinician.

A third part is harder to put in a sentence. Some patients arrive having been told once, somewhere, that their teeth were bad, and have organised their relationship with dentistry around that message ever since. Avoidance compounds: the longer it has been, the more there is to be embarrassed by, the more reasons not to go. Working chair-side, the most common thing a new patient says to me, almost word for word, is some version of: I'm sorry, I know it's bad.

Who falls through

The cost barrier hits hardest where you would expect it to. Across the practices I have worked in, the pattern is consistent: the patients who come in with the most extensive problems are usually those for whom the problem was not the teeth in isolation but the absence of a financial cushion that would have made earlier care possible. Sole parents. Workers in casualised employment. Older adults on superannuation. Recent migrants.

The data agrees. The 2009 New Zealand Oral Health Survey found that around 40% of adults reported they had avoided some kind of dental care due to cost. Subsequent work has shown that affordability has worsened, not improved, in the years since. A 2025 study in Community Dentistry and Oral Epidemiology traced the steady decline in dental affordability from 1978 to 2023 and found the gap widening across that whole period.

For Māori the inequity is structural. Māori adults carry roughly 10% more affected teeth than non-Māori; Māori children have 50% more affected primary teeth and 80% more affected permanent teeth. These are not differences that emerge from the chair. They are differences that walk into the chair.

What I have come to think

Two things, mostly.

The first is that oral health is treated as somehow separate from the rest of health policy in this country in a way that does not survive scrutiny. Severe dental disease is associated with cardiovascular disease, with diabetes complications, with pregnancy outcomes, with mental health outcomes. Untreated infection causes systemic illness. And yet, on the funding side of the system, the mouth begins at the lips and ends at the throat: a small territory carved out and left to the private market in a way no other part of the body is.

The second is that the workforce shortage and the access problem are not separate issues. They reinforce each other. Without sufficient capacity, even policies that fund care run into delivery problems. Without affordable access, the people who need care most often do not enter the system at all, which suppresses the visible demand that workforce planning is built on, which keeps the workforce at its current size. It is a system that produces its own status quo.

I am not arguing here for any one policy fix. Means-tested adult dental subsidies, expansion of dental therapist scope of practice, capitation funding for high-need populations, a publicly funded adult emergency care entitlement: there are many designs that would shift the picture meaningfully, and they each have trade-offs worth working through carefully. What I am arguing is that oral health belongs near the centre of the policy conversation, not at the edges of it, and that the analysis would be better if more of the people doing it had spent some time in the rooms where the system actually meets the people it is meant to serve.

~

Johanna Escudero Pino is a Wellington-based public health analyst. She holds a Postgraduate Diploma in Public Health from the University of Otago and has worked in Wellington dental practices since 2015.
